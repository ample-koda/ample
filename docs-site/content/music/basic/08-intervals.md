## What an interval is

Count the steps between two notes. A one-semitone step is a **minor second**. Seven semitones is a **perfect fifth**. Each interval has its own character, and you will start to recognise them.

| Interval | Semitones | Sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| minor second | 1 | tense, the Jaws theme |
| major second | 2 | the start of "Happy Birthday" |
| minor third | 3 | sad, dark |
| major third | 4 | bright, happy |
| perfect fourth | 5 | open, "Here comes the bride" |
| perfect fifth | 7 | strong, hollow, powerful |
| octave | 12 | the same note, higher |

{{deeper Consonance and dissonance}}
Some intervals sound stable (**consonant**), some sound tense (**dissonant**). The stable ones are those with simple frequency ratios: octave (2:1), fifth (3:2), fourth (4:3), thirds and sixths. The tense ones are seconds and sevenths, and the tritone (6 semitones).

Dissonance is not "bad". It creates movement: a tense interval wants to move to a stable one, and that release is much of what makes music feel like it goes somewhere. Pure consonance all the time is calm but also static.
{{/deeper}}

{{deeper Inverting an interval}}
Flip two notes (move the lower one up an octave) and the interval changes in a predictable way: the sizes add up to 9 for steps (a third becomes a sixth, a fourth becomes a fifth), and major turns minor. So a major third (C-E) becomes a minor sixth (E-C). This is why a major third and a minor sixth sound related.
{{/deeper}}
