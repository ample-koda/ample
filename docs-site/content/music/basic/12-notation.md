## The staff

Music is written on five lines, the **staff**. A note higher on the staff is a higher pitch. At the start a **clef** says which notes the lines are: the treble clef for high notes, the bass clef for low notes.

## Note heads and stems

- A **filled head** with a stem is a quarter note or shorter.
- An **open head** is a half or whole note.
- **Flags** and **beams** on the stem make notes shorter (eighth, sixteenth).
- A **dot** after a note makes it half as long again.

## Chords on paper

Above the staff many songs just write chord names (C, Am, F, G). A musician then plays the chord in their own way. This is the simplest way to write a song, and it is how AMPLE can store a song too.

## Is it necessary?

No. Many great musicians never read a note. What helps more is to hear: rhythm, pitch, the pull of a chord. That is why this course has sounds first. If you want to read music later, you will find that you already know what the dots mean.

{{deeper Key signatures}}
At the start of a staff, sharps or flats show which key the piece is in, so they do not need to be written on every note. No signs: C major or A minor. One sharp (F#): G major or E minor. One flat (Bb): F major or D minor. The order of sharps is F C G D A E B, and of flats the reverse.
{{/deeper}}

{{deeper Rhythm without pitch}}
Drums are often written on a single line: each note shows only when to hit, not which pitch. Different positions or note-head shapes show different drums (kick low, snare in the middle, hi-hat above with a cross). It reads the same way as the grids used in this course.
{{/deeper}}
