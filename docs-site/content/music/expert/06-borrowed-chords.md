## Borrowed chords

In C major the IV chord is F major. If you play F **minor** instead (borrowed from C minor) you get a sudden, bitter-sweet shade.

## Secondary dominants

Any chord can be treated as a temporary home. Approach it with its own dominant. D7 is the dominant of G, so D7 before G gives a stronger arrival.

## Key change

Move everything up by a step (or a few) for the last chorus. It feels like an extra lift.

{{deeper How to find the pivot}}
A smooth key change often uses a chord both keys share. Find a chord that exists in both, play it, and then treat it as a chord in the new key. The ear follows the common chord across.
{{/deeper}}

{{deeper Which chords can be borrowed}}
Take the parallel minor (same home note, minor scale). In C major, borrow from C minor:

| Borrowed | Chord | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| iv | Fm | bitter-sweet, the "movie" sad turn |
| bVII | Bb | rock, hopeful strength |
| bVI | Ab | epic, dramatic |
| bIII | Eb | bold, cinematic |

Use one borrowed chord for colour in an otherwise plain progression. Two in a row start to sound like a key change.
{{/deeper}}

{{deeper More on secondary dominants}}
You can approach any chord of the key (except the diminished one) with its own dominant seventh. G is V in C. D7 is V of G, written V7/V. A7 is V7/ii (it leads to Dm). Every such chord has one note outside the key, which makes the arrival feel stronger. In a chain, each chord pulls to the next (the "circle progression"): A7 - D7 - G7 - C.
{{/deeper}}

{{deeper Modulating cleanly}}
Ways to change key, from simple to elaborate:

1. **Direct**: just start the next section in the new key, often a step up. Works for pop.
2. **Pivot chord**: use a chord that belongs to both keys, then treat it as a chord in the new key.
3. **Dominant approach**: play the new key's dominant seventh, then land on its tonic.
4. **Common-tone**: keep one note that sits in both chords while the rest moves.
{{/deeper}}
