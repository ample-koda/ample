## Independent lines

Counterpoint is the art of combining two or more melodies. Each one should be singable, and together they should sound good.

**Motion between two voices**:

- **Parallel**: both go the same direction, same distance (strong, risks sounding empty with fifths and octaves).
- **Contrary**: one goes up while the other goes down (the most independent).
- **Oblique**: one holds while the other moves.

## Avoid and aim

Avoid two voices moving in parallel perfect fifths or octaves for long: they merge into one. Prefer thirds and sixths in parallel: they stay rich.

{{deeper The classic rules (and why they exist)}}
Counterpoint teaches a few rules, each with a reason:

- **Avoid parallel fifths and octaves.** Two voices that move in the same perfect interval fuse into one, so you lose the independence you wanted.
- **Prefer stepwise motion.** Small steps are easy to sing and follow.
- **After a leap, go back.** A jump creates tension; the next note should return inside the leap.
- **Start and end on stable intervals.** Octaves, fifths and thirds anchor the line.
- **Keep one voice moving while the other holds.** Variety gives the ear something to follow.

The rules are a starting point, not law: pop and jazz break many of them deliberately. Knowing them lets you break them with intention.
{{/deeper}}

{{deeper Bach chorales as a model}}
J. S. Bach wrote hundreds of four-part chorales, each with a soprano, alto, tenor and bass that are all good melodies. They show how harmony can emerge from independent lines. A good exercise is to take a simple tune, add a bass line that moves mostly in contrary motion, and then fill the middle with notes that fit the chords.
{{/deeper}}
