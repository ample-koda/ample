## Scotch snap

A short, accented note followed by a longer one: a sixteenth, then a dotted eighth. It sounds like a little "snap" and gives Scottish dance tunes their bounce. The reverse (long, then short) is the ordinary dotted rhythm.

`x x . .`  (snap: short then long)

`x . . x`  (dotted: long then short)

{{deeper Origin}}
The snap is the heart of the Scottish **strathspey**, a moderately slow dance in 4/4. In Baroque music the same figure is called the Lombard rhythm. Historians do not agree where it began: the earliest written strathspey-rhythm tune is "Macpherson's Testament" in a manuscript from 1710, and a snap shows up in a mid-17th-century Scottish tune. The usual account credits Speyside fiddlers of the 18th century, though some researchers find the snap already in older Gaelic work and dance songs.
{{/deeper}}

## Charleston

A dotted quarter followed by an eighth: one hit on beat 1 and one on the "and" of 2, then the same again. It is easy to recognise because the second hit lands just before beat 3 and pushes the music forward.

`x . . . . . x . x . . . . . x .`

{{deeper Origin}}
It is named after the Charleston dance, which swept the United States in the 1920s after the song "The Charleston" by James P. Johnson (1923, from the Broadway show *Runnin' Wild*). The dance and its rhythm are often linked to Black communities of the American South, and the pattern is a close relative of the tresillo.
{{/deeper}}
