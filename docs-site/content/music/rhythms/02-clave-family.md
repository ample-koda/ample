## Son clave

The son clave is five hits in one bar, written **3-2**: three hits in the first half, two in the second. Other instruments are built around it, so everything feels "locked" to it. Turn the halves around (2-3) and you get the reverse direction. A band picks one direction and keeps it for the whole song.

`x . . x . . x . . . x . x . . .`

{{deeper Origin}}
Named after the **claves**, two short hardwood sticks that are knocked together. The pattern comes from the Cuban *son*, a style that grew in the east of Cuba around the end of the 19th century and reached Havana in the early 20th. Its roots lie in West and Central African bell patterns. The Cuban historian Fernando Ortiz traced it to a 6/8 African bell pattern (see the Standard African Bell pattern, two pages on).
{{/deeper}}

## Rumba clave

The rumba clave is the son clave with one small change: the third hit comes one step **later**. That tiny delay makes it feel more open and a little more stretched.

{{deeper Origin}}
The rumba (the music, not the ballroom dance of the same name) developed among Afro-Cuban communities in Havana and Matanzas in the second half of the 19th century, with drums, voices and the clave.
{{/deeper}}

## Tresillo

Three hits grouped **3 + 3 + 2** eighth notes. It is the shortest pattern with a strong forward push, because the last gap is shorter than the others. Each step below is an eighth note.

`x . . x . . x .`

{{deeper Origin}}
*Tresillo* is Spanish for "triplet". The rhythm is found in the Cuban habanera and contradanza of the 19th century and travelled to the United States, where it shaped ragtime and early New Orleans jazz. Jelly Roll Morton called this Latin flavour the "Spanish tinge".
{{/deeper}}

## Bo Diddley beat

The same shape as the son clave 3-2, but played as a full groove: a guitar strums the clave pattern while a shaker keeps eighth notes going. It is the sound of "bomp, ba-bomp bomp, bomp".

`x . . x . . x . . . x . x . . .`

{{deeper Origin}}
It is named after the American guitarist Bo Diddley, who made it famous with his debut single "Bo Diddley" in 1955. It is described as a variation of the 3-2 clave. The street body-percussion tradition called *hambone*, where performers slap and pat their bodies while chanting rhymes, has been suggested as a source. You can hear the beat in "Not Fade Away" (Buddy Holly, 1957), "Panic in Detroit" (David Bowie, 1973) and "Desire" (U2, 1988).
{{/deeper}}

## Five beats: 3 + 2

In 5/4 there are five beats per bar, and nearly every 5/4 piece feels them in two groups: **3 + 2** (or 2 + 3). A clave-like pattern in five just makes that grouping obvious: a strong hit on beat 1 and another on beat 4.

`1 . . 4 .`

## A cousin you know: "Shave and a haircut, two bits"

A seven-note call and answer: **"Shave and a hair-cut"** (five hits) and, after a pause, **"two bits"** (two hits). In beats it is: "Shave" on 1, "and a" on 2 and the "and" of 2, "hair" on 3, "cut" on 4, then one beat of silence, "two" on 2 and "bits" on 3 of the next bar. The answer is played a little higher in the preview. Its rhythm is said to be akin to the Bo Diddley beat, which is why the knock-knock pattern feels so familiar.

{{pv {"title":"Shave and a haircut, two bits","src":"rhythm","p":{"bpm":120,"bars":2,"cycle":8,"layers":[{"n":91,"at":[0,1,1.5,2,3]},{"n":96,"at":[5,6]},{"n":67,"at":[0,1,2,3,4,5,6,7],"v":0.4}]}}}}

{{deeper Origin}}
One early appearance is in the introduction of Charles Hale's song "At a Darktown Cake Walk" (1899). The same notes appear in "Hot Scotch Rag" (1911) and in "On the 5:15" (1915). "Two bits" is slang for a quarter, 25 cents.
{{/deeper}}
