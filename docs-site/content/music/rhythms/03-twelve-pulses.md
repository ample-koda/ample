## The twelve-pulse grid

Most pop is "straight": a beat splits into **two**. If a beat splits into **three**, a bar has twelve pulses instead of eight, and the music rolls. The patterns below use that grid. Read each `x` or `.` as one pulse, three per beat.

## Standard African Bell pattern

Seven hits on twelve pulses. Count the gaps between hits and you get 2 2 1 2 2 2 1. The pattern splits into two opposed halves, which is why it is such a good guide for dancers and drummers.

`x . x . x x . x . x . x`

{{deeper Origin}}
Used in many West African traditions, especially Ewe and Yoruba music, where one bell player keeps this "guide pattern" while drums play around it. Scholars call it the *standard pattern* or Standard African Bell pattern; musicians know it as the *bembé bell* (from the Yoruba-Cuban bembé) or the *Agbekor bell* (from Ewe music in Ghana). The gaps 2 2 1 2 2 2 1 are the same as the steps of the major scale (whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half), a coincidence that fascinates rhythm researchers.
{{/deeper}}

## Shuffle

Every beat is a **long-short** pair: the first and third pulse of a triplet, with the middle one left out. It is the "chicka-chicka" of the blues.

{{deeper Origin}}
The shuffle is often traced to blues and boogie-woogie piano of the 1930s and 40s, where the left hand rolls out a steady long-short pattern. It carried into jump blues, Chicago blues and early rock and roll.
{{/deeper}}

## Swing

Swing is the shuffle's looser cousin. The jazz ride cymbal plays a quarter note on every beat and adds a skip note before beats 3 and 1 (the "ding, ding-a, ding, ding-a" pattern). The exact long-short ratio is not fixed: it is close to 2:1 at medium tempo and gets straighter when the music is fast.

{{deeper Origin}}
Swing grew out of 1920s jazz and gave its name to the big-band era of the 1930s and 40s. Duke Ellington summed it up in the title "It Don't Mean a Thing (If It Ain't Got That Swing)" (1931).
{{/deeper}}
